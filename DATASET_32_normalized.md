On space: a single padel court exceeds 200 m² once the safety perimeter is included (Racquet Sports Institute), with courts typically built to a standard 10×20 m playing footprint (data from 2025, newer figures not available) (FMG Consulting). F&B: even a small bar/lounge lifts dwell time and per-visit spend; padel is social and players linger. Often the difference between marginal and healthy EBITDA.
Leagues, tournaments, corporate/team-building events: fill courts in blocks and recruit new members.
Retail & stringing, racket/ball sales, equipment rental for newcomers (padel's beginner intake is high).
Court-side and digital advertising / sponsor naming of courts.
6. Key risks and assumptions to validate
Competitive saturation: Prague padel supply has grown fast; confirm current court count within a 15–20 min catchment of Vysočany before fixing prices. Oversupply collapses prime pricing power.
Catchment: Vysočany is a densely developing, transit-connected part of Prague 9 — favorable.
Indoor cost base: Czech energy and rent are lower than Western Europe but heating indoor courts in winter is a real cost — off-peak pricing must still clear marginal energy cost.
Seasonality: indoor demand peaks in winter; plan promotions for summer troughs.
Bottom line: Protect prime-time yield with dynamic pricing and fixed-slot season contracts, use targeted off-peak memberships purely to fill the daytime hole, keep casual play frictionless and pay-as-you-go, and treat coaching and F&B as core profit centers rather than afterthoughts. Branding a New Padel Club: Naming, Identity, and Positioning That Compound
The decision that matters most here is not the name itself — it is choosing, before you name anything, which of the eight recognisable positioning stances your club will actually run as, and then making sure the name, the visual identity, and the operating model all point at that same stance. A brand that says one thing and delivers another is the single most common and most expensive branding mistake in padel, and it shows up in the evidence repeatedly.
Pick the stance before you pick the name
Padel clubs communicate one of a small set of stances to the market: social/community, membership, utilisation (book-play-leave), coaching/academy, events/competition, hospitality/experience, embedded amenity (courts inside a bigger host offer), or public access. Each stance carries a coherence obligation — it has to be operationally true, not just written on the sign. "Social" is the cheapest word to claim and the hardest to actually run: it requires a paid, active community animator, social-first programming, and a genuinely comfortable social space, not a cold changing room and a booking app. A club that puts "community" in its name but has no one staffed to build that community has the label without the engine, and players notice the gap fast.
The evidence shows what coherent execution looks like across different stances. Powerhouse Padel in Coleraine built its identity around one visually ownable signature — purple courts, unusual in the UK — paired with in-club replay technology, and it opened into a market with no padel courts within 50 miles, so the brand's job there is category creation rather than differentiation against rivals (World Padel Network — Jamie Rowe). Los Angeles Padel Club is deliberately building a hospitality stance: a full-scale, hospitality-driven clubhouse experience aimed at long-term guest retention rather than pure court access (Racketsportsweekly.com — Ian Rapport). Hype Club Centro Padel in Trento leaned into a coaching stance, using FITP-certified pro coaches and a targeted university marketing push to visibly lift court occupancy within seven months of opening (Padel Business Magazine). Padel Pro built a multi-site brand around inclusive, welcoming community programming — beginner meetups, social tournaments, youth programmes — across Dubai, Jakarta and soon Abu Dhabi (World Padel Network — Jamie Rowe). Bela Padel Center in Buenos Aires is positioning as a premium club concept integrated into a next-generation, mixed-use real-estate development, with its identity built through scale and setting rather than through price competition (Padel Cluster).

## [14] — C2 Design/build

### [14] · RacqI-Claude

Padel architecture is not a scaled-down tennis brief. The playing box is fixed by the International Padel Federation at 20 metres by 10 metres, but almost everything that determines whether a facility works — structurally, commercially and socially — sits in the decisions made around that box: whether it sits under a roof, how the enclosure is engineered, where it is sited relative to neighbours, how players move through the building, and how many courts justify the fixed costs of running it at all. This report works through those decisions in the order a project actually has to make them: site and regulatory gate first, then structure and envelope, then court-level detail, then internal layout and flow, then building systems, and finally the sizing and cost model that ties the architecture to the P&L.
1. The site decision comes before the design brief
Location is not a downstream detail in padel — it is the first constraint that can kill a project outright, and the reason is noise. The Sports and Play Construction Association is unambiguous that padel courts have been shut down by environmental health authorities after construction, even where planning consent had already been granted, because of the intensity of noise generated by ball-on-glass and ball-on-racket impact (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)). Where a residential property sits within 30 metres of a proposed court, a formal acoustic assessment is advisable before a planning application is even submitted, and the local authority can require acoustic fencing, barriers, or — in the worst case — relocation of the court entirely; some authorities also reserve the right to demand a second acoustic assessment once the court is operating (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)). This has to be treated as a site-viability gate, assessed before a lease is signed or a design brief written, not as a design-review checkbox.
Beyond acoustics, site selection carries its own technical due diligence: topography, access, drainage, tree cover and — critically — ground conditions. Poor ground, high clay content or historical land contamination can make a site materially more expensive to develop, and SAPCA's guidance is candid that some sites simply are not cost-effective to build on once engineering costs are factored in (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)). A geotechnical survey before committing to a site is the standard risk-mitigation step for anything beyond the simplest, driest, flattest plot. Location also decides commercial reach in the more conventional sense — ease of access, visibility, and parking availability all shape footfall, and sun angle relative to court orientation is a basic playability check that costs nothing to get right at the siting stage (LEGEND SPORTS).
2. Indoor versus outdoor is a structural fork, not a style preference
The single most consequential early decision is whether the court will be indoor or outdoor, because it cascades into structure, glazing specification, foundation design, lighting strategy and permitting complexity simultaneously — it is not a finishing choice made late in design (Racquet Sports Institute).
Outdoor courts must be engineered against real wind loads, sun exposure and temperature swings. Structural calculations for outdoor courts — especially panoramic configurations, which by definition remove intermediate support posts — must be signed off by a competent structural engineer carrying professional indemnity insurance, reflecting local wind-loading conditions that vary considerably by region (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)). Metalwork typically needs galvanising or specialist anti-corrosion coatings, and European guidance points to reinforced steel profiles up to 4 mm thick for structural elements exposed to significant wind load (Racquet Sports Institute). Foundations follow the same logic: outdoor courts need a detailed geotechnical survey to determine soil stability, and the foundation itself is often deeper and T-shaped for stability, with drainage asphalt recommended in climates with frequent rain or freeze-thaw cycles (Racquet Sports Institute). Indoor courts, by contrast, are shielded from all of this — a straightforward concrete slab roughly 20 centimetres thick with a smooth, level screed finish is generally sufficient, since there is no wind load to resist (Racquet Sports Institute). Indoor construction shifts the burden instead onto ceiling height, ventilation and artificial lighting design, and typically triggers a heavier permitting load — structural engineering, fire safety and ventilation standards are all commonly required where a full enclosure or roof structure is involved (Racquet Sports Institute, Racquet Sports Institute).
The commercial logic strongly favours indoor outside genuinely warm-climate markets. In mature European markets, indoor padel courts sustain 70–90% peak occupancy against a 30–50% off-season collapse for outdoor equivalents, and the difference is explicitly attributed to predictable playing conditions rather than lifestyle aesthetics — Italy has already crossed 45% indoor inventory nationally, and colder markets such as the UK and Northern Europe are now essentially indoor-only by necessity (Racquet Sports Institute — Markus Gaebel). The same evidence is candid that Sweden's well-documented padel collapse was in significant part an indoor-execution failure — cheap covered structures with poor lighting and poor acoustics, and no hospitality layer — rather than a failure of the indoor model itself (Racquet Sports Institute — Markus Gaebel). The lesson for architecture is that "indoor" buys you occupancy resilience only if the shell is built to a decent specification; a bare warehouse box with a roof is not the same product as an indoor club.
A mixed indoor/outdoor format — covered courts for winter, uncovered for the warm season — is a common third path in transitional climates, trading a larger footprint and higher capital cost for better amortisation of fixed costs through higher year-round court rotation, and Italian market guidance treats it as the most flexible but also the most land- and capital-intensive format (lexpress-franchise.com).
3. Court-level architecture: enclosure, glazing and surface
Whatever the envelope decision, the court itself carries a tight technical specification that the architect must accommodate rather than improvise. The enclosure back walls need 3 metres of solid, uniform-rebound material (glass or brick) topped by 1 metre of mesh, for a 4-metre total enclosure height. Twelve-millimetre tempered glass, kitemarked, is now the de facto standard for any outdoor or canopy-covered court; 10 mm remains acceptable only for fully indoor courts, and even then only with intermediate support posts rather than a panoramic, post-free configuration (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA), Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)). Access points are typically hinged, positioned on one or both sides depending on the court's relationship to circulation routes, and every multi-court scheme needs enough spacing between courts for player and sports-wheelchair access, in line with disability-access obligations under the Equality Act 2010 in the UK context, or equivalent accessibility law elsewhere (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA), Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)).
Playing surface has converged strongly on fibrillated artificial turf — ribbon-like fibres roughly 10–12 mm high, with a silica sand infill of around 8–10 kg/m² brushed in until invisible — because it produces no dust indoors, needs minimal maintenance, and works in both indoor and outdoor settings; monofilament turf is the older, dustier and higher-maintenance alternative, and painted concrete or acrylic-coated hard surfaces are now effectively obsolete for competitive play (Racquet Sports Institute). Whether the court should be single or double width is worth flagging explicitly for anyone new to the format: single courts change the pace and character of the game enough that they are not a substitute for a doubles court in a commercial booking model, and their real value is as a dedicated one-on-one coaching space, chosen only where footprint genuinely will not accommodate a second doubles court (Racquet Sports Institute).
Whether the facility should plan now for a future roof is a decision that has to be made at the masterplanning stage, not retrofitted — covering a previously open court later can significantly change the layout, the canopy or indoor structure, and the footprint required, so the "future-proofing" question belongs in the initial brief (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)).
4. Internal layout, zoning and player flow
Once the court block is fixed, the rest of the building has to be sized and arranged around it deliberately rather than left as leftover space. The working rule from facility-design benchmarking is that total facility area should be built up as the sum of court footprints plus an infrastructure allowance per court — roughly 80–100 m² per court — covering everything that is not the court itself: arrival and reception, changing and welfare, social and F&B space, programme space, and back-of-house. This allowance is treated as constant per court regardless of sport, because support-space demand scales with the number of players the courts generate — court count — not with how large any individual court is. Lean, booking-led concepts sit at the lower end of that range; premium, social-led concepts add social and F&B space on top of it.
Applying that logic concretely: an Italian market guide gives a working footprint of roughly 12 by 22 metres per court once the safety perimeter and fencing are included — that is 264 m² of court-plus-buffer area per court (lexpress-franchise.com). Add the 80 m²/court infrastructure allowance and each court effectively needs 344 m² of total facility area. For a six-court facility — the low end of what the evidence identifies as standalone-viable scale for padel (more on this below) — that comes to 2,064 m² of gross facility area, which is the sizing anchor the cost model below is built on.
Circulation and flow deserve as much attention as square metreage. The naive design — the shortest path from arrival to changing to court — is a commercial mistake in any racquet facility: routing players past social, F&B and retail space on the way to and from the court measurably increases spend, and changing rooms are best placed deep in the plan rather than immediately off reception, provided front-desk sightlines still allow adequate supervision. Padel-specific guidance reinforces the same point in plainer language — leave free, wide walkways and space for workers and safety egress around the court block, and position any spectator seating so it doesn't obstruct player sightlines or block emergency routes (LEGEND SPORTS). Every racquet sport, padel included, benefits from social sightlines to the courts regardless of whether the concept is positioned as booking-led or premium-social — a bar or lounge that overlooks live play consistently outperforms one tucked into a blind corner, because watching the game is itself part of the product.
For multi-court schemes specifically, adequate inter-court spacing is not optional: it has to accommodate player and sports-wheelchair movement and comply with disability-access law, and the "out-of-court" play-area question — extra buffer space for competition-level play — needs to be resolved at design stage, because retrofitting it once a facility is built is disruptive and expensive (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA), Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)).
5. Lighting, ventilation and building systems
Lighting in padel does double duty as a playability requirement and a planning-compliance issue. Recreational and club play is comfortably served by around 200 lux of horizontal illuminance; competitive or tournament use needs 500 lux or more, with high uniformity so there are no dark patches on court (Racquet Sports Institute). Outdoor courts are typically lit by four floodlight poles, roughly 6 metres high, positioned outside the enclosure so fixtures cannot interfere with high shots; indoor courts more commonly use suspended LED fixtures. Because padel players are constantly looking upward for lobs and overhead shots, glare control is a genuine safety and playability issue, not an aesthetic one — asymmetric optics and visor shields that direct light onto the court while cutting upward and outward spill are standard specification, alongside a colour temperature in the 3,000–4,000 K (or up to 6,500 K for some commercial systems) range for good ball contrast (Racquet Sports Institute, LEGEND SPORTS). On the compliance side, many municipalities cap light trespass beyond the property line and require lights off by a set hour; a photometric report submitted alongside the planning application, showing cut-off shields and appropriate pole heights, materially smooths approval and heads off resident objections before they become a post-completion enforcement problem (Racquet Sports Institute).
Indoor projects carry an additional, easily underestimated systems requirement: sufficiently high ceilings and good air movement, since glass-enclosed courts otherwise trap heat and humidity (LEGEND SPORTS). Italian regulatory guidance specifies a minimum internal clear height of 8–10 metres for indoor courts to allow the ball to fly correctly during play, and treats this as a hard planning parameter rather than a design preference (lexpress-franchise.com).
6. Sizing the facility to the economics — scale is an architectural input, not an afterthought
The architecture cannot be sensibly sized without first confronting the commercial scale question, because in padel the building's fixed costs — reception, changing rooms, booking systems, insurance, management — are largely indifferent to how many courts sit behind them. Industry benchmarking is consistent that padel needs roughly five to six courts for standalone commercial viability, with six to ten courts representing the optimal range; a four-court facility at moderate US pricing generates only around $294,000 in annual court revenue against $270,000-plus in fixed operating costs — barely break-even — while the same pricing model at six courts yields roughly $588,000, creating a meaningful margin (Raquet Sports Institute). The same source quantifies the "4-court trap" directly: at three to four courts, operating cost per court runs $133,000–$183,000 against moderate revenue per court of $100,000–$167,000 — breakeven or a loss — while at six courts, operating cost per court falls to $138,000–$165,000 against revenue per court of $167,000–$250,000, a 15–35% margin, and at ten-plus courts the margin widens to 35–40% as fixed costs are spread across more revenue-generating capacity (Raquet Sports Institute). These figures are US-market and dollar-denominated in the source evidence; they are not directly interchangeable with the euro-denominated build-cost figures below without a stated exchange-rate assumption, but the pattern — margin improving sharply with court count — is the architecturally relevant takeaway regardless of currency or market.
The practical implication for design is to treat five to six courts as the minimum sensible unit to draw up, and to build the structure so growing beyond that unit later is cheap. A padel-dimensioned column grid — roughly 11–12 by 21 metres, columns only at the bay corners — lets every other racquet sport (pickleball, badminton, squash) fit inside the same structural bay without touching the frame, and sits well below the roughly 30-metre span at which heavy steel becomes materially more expensive. Baking that grid into the shell from day one is what makes phased expansion, or a later change of sport mix, a re-lining exercise rather than a construction project.

Putting a number on the architecture matters because the building envelope, not the court kit, is usually the largest single line in a padel project's capital cost — and it is the line most often understated in back-of-envelope investor pitches. Verdict
Padel architecture works when six decisions are made in the right order and none of them is treated as optional: confirm the site clears the noise gate before committing capital; decide indoor versus outdoor as a structural and commercial choice, not a budget afterthought, recognising that indoor generally wins on occupancy resilience outside warm-climate markets provided the shell specification is not cut to the bone; get the enclosure, glazing and surface spec right because it is largely non-negotiable under FIP and safety standards; design internal layout and circulation to route players past monetisable space rather than around it; specify lighting and ventilation as compliance issues as much as playability ones; and size the whole building to a court count — five to six courts minimum — that can actually absorb its own fixed costs, using a padel-dimensioned structural grid so that scale can be added later without touching the frame. Executed in that order, a padel facility is a sound and increasingly proven built-asset class across most of Europe; executed out of order — site chosen before the noise question is asked, or a four-court shell built to keep initial capital low — the building itself becomes the constraint that caps the business.

Recommendation: Design around court utilization economics first, everything else second. Your facility lives or dies on peak-hour occupancy and off-peak cost control. Optimize the layout to maximize sellable court-hours, minimize the staff and energy needed to run them, and protect the premium (prime-time) inventory that generates most of your revenue.
1. Court count, orientation, and structure
Court count sets your economics. Below ~4 courts, fixed costs (reception, changing rooms, management) are hard to cover. The common sweet spot is 4–6 courts for a standalone club; 8+ if you're building a destination/social venue with F&B. More courts also smooth revenue against maintenance downtime.
Standard court dimensions are 20m × 10m playing area, but plan for ~11m × 20m per court including surrounding structure and safety runoff, and add circulation space between courts. Panoramic (glass-side) courts command higher rates and photograph better for marketing, but cost more.
Orientation matters for outdoor/semi-open courts: align the long axis roughly north–south so the low sun doesn't blind players looking down the court at sunrise/sunset. Indoor courts avoid this but incur lighting and climate costs.
Structure type is the biggest capital fork:
Indoor (fixed building/warehouse conversion): highest capex, but weatherproof, year-round revenue, premium pricing. Requires ~8–9m clear height above the court (ball goes high in padel — insufficient roof clearance is a common, expensive mistake).
Covered/canopy structures (steel + membrane): lower cost, faster to erect, but climate control is limited.
Outdoor: cheapest, but weather-dependent utilization kills the business case in most of continental Europe outside summer.
2. Clear height and lighting
Minimum clear height is roughly 8m over the whole court; more is better for play quality. Skimping here is irreversible and degrades the product.
Lighting should be uniform LED (target commonly cited around 300–500 lux for recreational/club play), positioned to avoid glare into players' eyes and shadows on the glass. LED is non-negotiable now — it's the single largest controllable operating cost lever after climate, and payback vs. older systems is typically fast.
3. Player flow and the "social sequence"
Padel is a social, 4-player sport, and the building should choreograph that. The high-value path: arrive → check in → change → play → shower → linger (bar/lounge) → leave. Design so players naturally pass the F&B and pro-shop on the way in and out — this is where secondary spend is captured.
Practical flow points:
Reception with clear sightlines to entrance and, ideally, to courts.
Changing rooms and showers sized for peak turnover — with 4 players per court arriving and leaving in overlapping 90-minute cycles, undersized facilities create bottlenecks and complaints.
Spectator/waiting/lounge space adjacent to courts. Padel's watchability is a genuine asset; glass courts + seating + a bar turns waiting time into revenue.
Wide circulation and clear wayfinding so overlapping sessions don't collide.
4. F&B and ancillary revenue
Court fees alone are a thin business. Mature operators increasingly treat padel as a hospitality venue with courts attached. Budget space for a bar/café, pro-shop, and possibly coaching/academy areas from day one — retrofitting is costly. Ancillary and F&B spend is what separates high-margin clubs from break-even ones.
5. Running costs — design them down now
The three big operating costs are energy (lighting + heating/cooling), staff, and maintenance. Design decisions lock these in:
Energy: LED lighting, good insulation on indoor builds, zoned climate control so you're not conditioning empty courts, and consider solar if roof area allows. Climate control in a high-volume indoor hall is a major cost — model it before committing to full air-conditioning vs. ventilation.
Staff: automated booking/access systems (app-based entry, keypad court access) allow off-peak unmanned operation — a significant labor saving that must be designed in (access control, cameras, lighting-on-booking).
Maintenance: artificial turf and glass are consumables. Turf typically needs replacing every few years under heavy use; specify quality turf and infill, and plan drainage (even indoors, for cleaning) and easy access for resurfacing.
6. Location and site constraints (design-shaping)
Ceiling height availability often dictates whether an existing building is even viable — check before you fall in love with a site.
Parking: 4 players × multiple courts turning over creates heavy, spiky parking demand. Undersized parking suppresses utilization.
Acoustics: padel is loud (ball-on-glass, shouting). Noise complaints are a leading cause of planning disputes and forced closures near residential areas. Budget for acoustic treatment/isolation and check local noise limits.
7. Permits and regulation 
Requirements vary by jurisdiction, so treat these as prompts to confirm with local authorities and a specialist, not as settled facts:
Change-of-use / planning permission (especially industrial-to-leisure conversions).
Building and fire safety, occupancy, and accessibility compliance for public assembly.
Noise/environmental permits — often the binding constraint near housing.
Structural sign-off for the roof/canopy loads.

The highest-value next step is a demand-and-utilization model for your specific site: it determines court count, structure type, and whether the F&B-led or courts-led design is right.

## [15] — C3 Acoustics

### [15] · RacqI-Claude

Acoustic Design for Padel Facilities: Challenges and Mitigation
Padel occupies an unusual acoustic position among racquet sports. It is not simply "loud" — measured overall sound levels are only moderately higher than tennis — but its noise character is what makes it a genuine planning and design risk, one that has already led to court closures and contested planning appeals in several markets (Sports and Play Construction Association (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)); The Padel Paper). Getting the acoustics right is now a first-order design decision, not an afterthought bolted on after the layout is fixed.
1. Why padel's noise profile is different from other racquet sports
The starting point for any padel acoustic design is understanding what actually generates the sound and how the enclosed court geometry shapes its behaviour. Padel's rules and construction encourage long rallies, and the ball is regularly played off the enclosure walls and mesh, so a court produces frequent additional impact noise beyond the racquet-ball strike itself (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)). It is this constant, varied series of sharp transient sounds, rather than sustained volume, that defines padel's noise character and makes it read as more intrusive than an equivalent average decibel level would suggest (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)).
Measured from the side of the court at the net line, a typical game of padel runs about 6 decibels louder than tennis on both average (LAeq) and peak (LAmax) metrics — a difference large enough to register as a clearly perceptible increase in loudness (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)). Measured from the enclosed glass end of the court, however, padel and tennis are almost identical, because the solid glass boundary acts as an effective barrier that blocks sound from escaping in that direction (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)). That containment is not free: the glass reflects the blocked sound back into the court, building up internal sound energy that then escapes through the far less effective mesh fencing along the sides. The net effect is that side-of-court noise runs roughly 12 decibels higher than at the glass end — more than double the perceived loudness — and the space between two parallel courts is consistently the loudest point in any layout, since it benefits from a semi-reverberant environment created between the facing enclosures (MW Acoustic Consultants (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)); MW Acoustic Consultants (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd))).
This geometry-driven asymmetry is the single most important fact for a designer to internalise: the open mesh sides, not the enclosed glass ends, carry the real acoustic risk to neighbours and to adjacent zones inside the building (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)). Standard noise assessments built around average overall levels (LAeq) routinely understate this risk because they do not capture the frequency and impulsive character of the impact sounds that drive actual complaints (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)).
2. The outdoor community-noise challenge
For any outdoor or open-sided padel installation, the dominant acoustic problem is neighbour disturbance, and it is now a well-documented cause of facility failure. Padel courts have been shut down through environmental health enforcement after construction, and high-profile planning appeals and community objections have followed the sport's rapid growth, particularly where courts sit close to residential property (Sports and Play Construction Association (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)); The Padel Paper).
The evidence available does not set a single fixed setback distance or a national decibel threshold for padel — and that gap is itself a finding: current UK planning guidance lacks specific daytime criteria for impact noise, the time of day padel is predominantly played, which means every site's context has to be assessed on its own terms against local ordinance rather than against a fixed national figure (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)). What the evidence does establish clearly is the trigger for concern: SAPCA expects an acoustic assessment to normally support a padel planning application given the increased noise from court construction, and specifically recommends acoustic reports wherever adjacent residential properties are considered noise-sensitive (Sports and Play Construction Association (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)); Sports and Play Construction Association (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA))). Some local authorities are now scrutinising padel applications and insisting on noise impact assessments more broadly than in padel's early growth years, reflecting a run of negative media attention and high-profile appeals (The Padel Paper).
Orientation is the single most important, and cheapest, design lever available. Because the glass ends behave as effective barriers and the mesh sides do not, the practical rule is to orient the solid glass ends of the court toward the nearest noise-sensitive neighbour and keep the open mesh sides facing away from residential receptors wherever the site allows it (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)). Where orientation cannot fully solve the problem — tight urban plots, multiple sensitive receptors on different sides — perimeter acoustic barriers become the next lever: solid walls, earth berms, or acoustic-fence products applied to a chain-link perimeter can meaningfully reduce the level reaching a boundary, provided the barrier actually breaks the line of sight between source and receiver — a low fence that leaves the upper mesh panel exposed does little. The exact height, setback distance and decibel target for any given site are matters for the commissioned acoustician to size against the local ordinance and the specific receptors, not a fixed industry number; the evidence reviewed does not support a single universal spec here.
3. The indoor in-hall challenge: reverberation between and within courts
Indoors, the same glass enclosure that contains outdoor noise becomes the source of a different problem: reverberation. A padel hall built from reflective glass boxes with hard floors and ceilings will tend toward excessive reverberation if left untreated, particularly where multiple courts sit side by side under one roof — exactly the configuration the loudest measured point (the gap between two parallel courts) describes outdoors, and the same geometry indoors traps and recirculates sound rather than letting it dissipate (MW Acoustic Consultants (a technical division of May Walters Ltd)).
The overhead plane is usually the largest uninterrupted reflective surface over any court and, left as bare concrete soffit or exposed metal roof deck, is typically the biggest in-hall acoustic mistake a design can make. The corrective is straightforward in principle: specify an absorptive ceiling treatment — acoustic baffles or clouds, or a treated soffit — as the primary in-hall lever, since an absorptive ceiling does not worsen the reverberation problem while a bare hard one actively compounds it. Wall-mounted absorption panels, or purpose-built products, supplement the ceiling treatment. This is precisely the market gap that specialist suppliers are now filling: one Sweden-based company, Padel Acoustics, specialises in soundproofing products installed on club partitions or between courts, with completed projects across Europe, South America, North America and the Middle East, positioning itself as an industry leader in this niche (PadelAddict — Padel Addict). The existence of a dedicated supplier market of this kind is itself evidence that acoustics has moved from an afterthought to a stated commercial consideration for serious operators, though specific claimed performance ratings or booking/revenue outcomes from any one supplier should be treated as directional marketing claims rather than independently audited figures. The evidence reviewed does not carry a specific reverberation-time or absorption-coefficient benchmark for padel halls, so exact targets should be set by the commissioned acoustician against the hall's actual volume and finishes.
4. Transmission to social areas and adjacent occupied spaces
The third acoustic problem is distinct from both the outdoor and in-hall cases: how sound generated at the court transmits into social, lounge, office or other occupied spaces that share the same building. Padel facilities face a genuine design tension here. Modern commercial padel depends on keeping the court visually connected to the social and F&B space — watching play from the bar or lounge is a real commercial draw regardless of a club's positioning — yet the court block needs to be acoustically separated from those same spaces to protect comfort and conversation.
The resolution is to separate the acoustic problem from the visual one rather than trying to solve both with the same element. Partition walls between the court block and noise-sensitive occupied space need airborne sound isolation sufficient for the adjacent use (conversation, calls, quiet lounge seating), with all air gaps sealed — an unsealed gap defeats an otherwise well-specified wall almost entirely. Glazed partitions are the practical answer to the sightline requirement: they provide airborne separation from the court noise while preserving the visual connection that makes the social space valuable — the isolation comes from the glazing and partition detailing, and the social value comes from keeping the sightline, so the two goals do not have to compete for the same design element. No facility-specific benchmark for the exact wall-rating target, the glazing specification, or the construction assembly suited to a given padel social-zone layout was found in the evidence reviewed for this answer, so that detail should be sized by the commissioned acoustician against the specific adjacency and occupancy in question rather than treated as fixed.
5. Assessment, regulation and the "get it in early" principle
A recurring theme across the evidence is that noise impact assessments (NIAs) done well, and done early, are the mechanism that actually protects a project — not a generic reassurance that "padel is only a bit louder than tennis." A good NIA compares the new and existing ambient noise environments and demonstrates both overall and impact-noise levels sit within acceptable limits against the relevant local guidance, rather than quoting a single overall decibel figure with no reference to where it was measured relative to the court (The Padel Paper). That last point matters enormously given the roughly 12 dB swing between the glass end and the open side of a court: an assessment that does not specify measurement position can be wrong by a wide margin before any other source of error is considered (The Padel Paper). The two most common failure modes in submitted assessments are exactly this — failing to compare padel's impulsive character against the existing impulsive/impact noise baseline at the site, and relying on unlocated single-figure decibel claims (The Padel Paper).
SAPCA's Code of Practice reflects this directly: an acoustic assessment is normally required to support a padel planning application once increased noise from court construction is acknowledged, and SAPCA specifically recommends acoustic reports wherever adjacent residential properties are considered noise-sensitive (Sports and Play Construction Association (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)); Sports and Play Construction Association (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA))). SAPCA also flags, pointedly, that even where a noise assessment has been made and agreed as part of planning consent, post-construction complaints and environmental health scrutiny can still follow — planning approval on paper does not retire the acoustic risk once the courts are operating (Sports and Play Construction Association (SAPCA)). Bringing an acoustician in at the design stage, before the layout and orientation are fixed, is repeatedly identified as the point of maximum leverage: input at that stage can steer court orientation and barrier placement in ways that are far cheaper than retrofitting mitigation once the courts are built and complaints have started (The Padel Paper).

| Sealed, sufficiently isolating partitions | Court block ↔ social/office/residential | Airborne isolation without blocking the commercially valuable sightline; rating to be set by the commissioned acoustician |
| Glazed partitions | Court block ↔ social space | Resolves the acoustic-vs-sightline trade-off directly |
| Early, position-specific noise impact assessment | All outdoor and boundary-adjacent sites | Locks orientation and barrier decisions before construction, not after complaints |
7. Practical implication and what to watch
The structural takeaway is that padel acoustics is not one problem but three, and each has its own fix: neighbour noise is solved primarily by orientation and setback before a single wall is specified; in-hall reverberation is solved primarily by ceiling absorption; and transmission into social space is solved by isolating the partition rather than the sightline. None of these are exotic interventions — they are largely a matter of sequencing the acoustic consultant's input to precede, not follow, the layout decision. Facilities that treat acoustics as a late-stage retrofit are the ones showing up in the closure and litigation cases; facilities that treat it as a first-stage design input are increasingly citing it as a commercial and reputational advantage.
What would sharpen this further: a site-specific noise-propagation model run against the actual proposed orientation and nearest receptors (the kind MW Acoustic Consultants and similar specialists build), and — for the social-zone partition question and for exact setback/barrier dimensions specifically — a project-level acoustic brief rather than general bands, since no padel-specific numeric benchmark for those elements was available in the evidence reviewed. Confidence in the comparative dB figures cited (the 6 dB side-differential and 12 dB side-versus-end gap) is medium: they come from a single, if credible, UK research study, and UK planning guidance itself still lacks impact-noise-specific daytime criteria, meaning site context will always move the applicable numbers.

### [15] · Nudo Claude (Opus 4.8)

Recommendation: Treat noise as a siting-and-permitting risk first, an engineering problem second. The dominant issue in padel is the sharp, impulsive "crack" of the ball on the carbon racquet (peaks around 100–115 dB at source, per acoustic consultancy measurements cited in UK and Nordic planning disputes). It is the impulsive, tonal character — not average sound levels — that generates complaints and drives planning refusals, especially for outdoor and canopy courts near residential receptors. Design your acoustics around that reality: locate defensively, then attenuate.
The three problem interfaces:
Court-to-outside (the deal-breaker). This is where padel projects get refused or shut down. The racquet impact is a broadband impulsive event with strong high-frequency content that carries and is highly noticeable against low background levels, particularly evenings and weekends when courts are busiest and neighbours are home. Most complaints come from open-air and lightweight-canopy installations.
Court-to-court. Impact noise and player shouting bleed between adjacent courts, degrading play and the premium feel. Glass back walls (structural to the game) reflect rather than absorb, so a naked multi-court hall is acoustically live.
Court-to-social area. Reverberation makes bars, lounges and reception fatiguing and hard to converse in — a retention and F&B-spend issue more than a compliance one.
Design solutions, in order of leverage:
Siting and layout. The cheapest dB is distance and orientation. Maximise separation to the nearest noise-sensitive receptor, orient court openings away from dwellings, and use the building's own massing (back-of-house, plant rooms, storage) as a barrier between courts and boundaries. For mixed indoor/outdoor sites, put outdoor courts on the far side from residents.
Fully enclosed construction for anything near receptors. A solid, sealed building with adequate mass (masonry or insulated sandwich panels rather than open canopies) is the single most reliable control for the outside interface. Acoustic louvres and lobbied/self-closing doors preserve ventilation and access without an open acoustic path — open doors and roller shutters routinely undo an otherwise compliant envelope.
Internal absorption. Because glass walls and hard floors can't be changed, put absorption where you can: acoustic panels or baffles on the ceiling and any non-glazed upper wall areas, and absorptive treatment above the glass line. This cuts reverberation, court-to-court bleed, and social-area harshness simultaneously.
Barriers and screening for outdoor/hybrid courts. Where full enclosure isn't feasible, use acoustic fencing or earth bunds along the receptor-facing boundary, and absorptive-lined perimeter walls. Effectiveness depends on breaking line-of-sight between the ball-strike height and the receptor.
Social-area separation. Acoustically rate the partition between courts and F&B/lounge, use lobbies rather than direct openings, and treat the social space itself for reverberation.
Operational controls as backstop. Enforced hours (evening/weekend restrictions are the most common planning condition), keeping perimeter openings shut, and managing outdoor-court scheduling.
Non-negotiable process point: commission a noise-impact assessment from an acoustician before you commit to a site or court mix. UK cases increasingly assess padel against the impulsive/tonal penalties in BS 4142 (industrial/commercial noise rating), and several outdoor schemes have been refused or conditioned on that basis — the impulsive character attracts a penalty that pushes the rating above thresholds even where average levels look benign. Confirm the equivalent local standard in your jurisdiction, because "acceptable on paper" using average metrics is exactly the trap that gets padel projects rejected.

### [16] · RacqI-Claude

Upselling and Cross-Selling at a Padel Club: What Works, and Where in the Journey
Assumption stated up front and carried from the compact answer: a standalone commercial padel club, positioning unspecified. Where the answer diverges between a membership-led and a utilisation-led model, both readings are given rather than one picked silently.
1. The core recommendation
Coaching pathways and membership tiering are the two upsell levers that reliably move padel revenue. Events and sponsorship sit second — they monetise capacity and community you already own. F&B and merchandise come last: they follow footfall rather than create it, and a premium peak slot is a condition to be earned through utilisation, not a default price move. This ordering is not a matter of taste. It follows from how padel revenue actually restructures as a club scales, and from where in the customer journey a player's willingness to spend is highest.
The structural argument against leading with hardware or a premium tariff is made bluntly by practitioners: the strategic question is whether a premium court alone produces a sustainable return, and in markets such as Spain the answer is increasingly no — a premium court only makes economic sense if it lets you charge significantly more, extends year-round usability, or forms part of a hospitality model that raises ancillary revenue and customer lifetime value (Padel Business Magazine). Courts are the centrepiece, but the value is generated in everything that surrounds them (Padel Business Magazine).
2. Scale sets the ceiling on the upsell mix
Before designing any offer, read the club's court count, because it determines which streams can physically exist. At three to four courts, court rentals are 55–70% of revenue and the diversified streams barely exist; at ten or more courts, rentals fall to 25–35% as coaching contributes 30–40%, F&B 5–10%, events 5–10% and retail 3–5%. In other words, the non-rental block moves from a rounding error to roughly 43–65% of the top line, and the rental share drops by about 35 percentage points across that range. The margin consequence is equally sharp: EBITDA moves from sub-20% at three to four courts to 35–40% at ten-plus, because programming density — leagues, coaching blocks, corporate formats — needs parallel courts to exist at all.
Two practical thresholds follow. Recreational leagues need at least three courts to run parallel fixtures rather than a queue. A staffed café needs roughly 150–200+ daily visitors, and impulse retail roughly 500+ unique weekly player visits — both implying six or more courts. Below six courts, a prep kitchen with simple snacks and externally-catered events is almost always the right call instead of a full F&B operation; grip, balls and a replacement racquet are the honest limit of the pro shop, because online price competition undermines full retail as a planned revenue stream.
| Court count | Where the upsell money is | What to hold back |
| 3–4 | Coaching, box ladder, one social night, rolling membership | F&B beyond vending/prep kitchen; retail; premium tariff |
| 6–8 | Coaching academy, leagues, corporate blocks, tiered membership, first sponsorship | Full restaurant, deep retail range |
| 10+ | Full programming stack, events as a product, sponsorship inventory, staffed F&B | Nothing structural — the constraint becomes demand, not scale |
3. The journey, touchpoint by touchpoint
Trial and first visit — the highest-yield moment in the whole funnel. The first coaching session, sold or bundled at the point of first booking, is what converts a court renter into a member of a community. The mechanism is human, not digital: staff training is what builds an inclusive hospitality culture that maximises loyalty and income from players who would otherwise just rent a court (Padel Business Magazine), and clubs that succeed plan that cultural layer from day one rather than adding it once the courts are full (Padel Business Magazine). Pair the intro lesson with level-based groups, an internal ladder and a small number of social sessions — deliberately low-tech matchmaking — so that players integrate, find compatible partners and build a pathway toward league play (Padel Business Magazine).
Booking flow — the yield and cross-sell engine, with a caveat. Modern platforms are revenue-management tools, data engines and community builders: they raise occupancy through real-time availability, automated waiting lists and level-based matchmaking, support dynamic pricing, manage coaching, leagues and events, and significantly increase average spend per customer (Padel Business Magazine). Their most strategic use is identifying the club's most valuable customers — those who play and spend most and bring friends — so selective incentives and exclusive benefits can be designed for them (Padel Business Magazine). The caveat is ownership of the relationship: some platforms pull the communication and data relationship away from the club, and in saturated markets players already feel more loyalty to their app than to any venue, at which point the club becomes interchangeable (Padel Business Magazine). Run club-branded leagues and direct communication alongside the platform, not instead of it. A comparable discipline is visible in a non-profit racquet operator that tracks the member journey from a social-media click through to court booking to personalise engagement — a membership of 450 with about 25% receiving discounts, and a majority of weekly court slots allocated to recreational play (data from 2024; newer figures not available) (Racquet Sports Institute).
Post-play window — F&B, retail and the social dwell time. This is where secondary spend lands, and it is a function of how long people choose to stay: the more time people pass in the club, the more money they spend there (Padel Business Magazine). Clubs that frame padel as a lifestyle experience — saunas, bars, social spaces and shops alongside premium courts in London, or steam room, fitness studio and lounge in New York — find that customers accept higher price points and stay longer (Padel Business Magazine). Treat that as an argument for designing dwell time into the building, not as licence to staff a kitchen at four courts.
Renewal and upgrade — where recurring revenue is won. Tiered plans with limited peak-hour priority for premium members, plus discounts and incentives to encourage off-peak play, are the practical form of creative pricing (Padel Business Magazine). Payment cadence matters as much as price: parents of younger players show strong tolerance for smaller recurring payments rather than lump sums, and clubs adopting rolling monthly or quarterly memberships reduce churn by 15%–25%, provided membership includes benefits beyond court access such as training, digital content and event discounts (Padel Business Magazine). A working reference point from Trento bundles discounted court rentals at set times and booking priority, training and nearby-spa discounts, and an annual premium booking-platform subscription (Padel Business Magazine). For a membership-led club, the target ratio is 80–150 active members per court, with above 200 signalling bottleneck risk; for a utilisation-led club the ratio is largely meaningless and court-hours sold is the metric to manage.
Advocacy — referral, events and sponsorship. A welcoming culture gives pricing power, encourages renewals and prompts players to invite friends, which lowers churn and raises lifetime value far more efficiently than constantly refilling the funnel with new trial users (Padel Business Magazine). Sponsorship is the most underused cross-sell: clubs positioning themselves as community hubs and lifestyle destinations attract non-endemic brands from fashion and automotive to finance and wellness, across corporate tournaments, leagues and open days, kit deals, digital visibility in booking apps, naming rights and branded courts (Padel Business Magazine). Brand partnerships also work commercially in reverse — a UK multi-site operator carries apparel, snack, beer and spirits partners whose products are sold in club (Padel Business Magazine).
4. Premium slots: earn the premium, then charge it
Peak — weekday evenings roughly 17:00–21:00 plus weekend mornings — carries around 40% of weekly demand and should be running at 70–85% utilisation before a premium tariff is layered on, out of roughly 98 available court-hours per week per court. Persistent readings below 50–60% at should-be-peak are a demand or pricing failure, not a pricing opportunity; consistently above 85–90% is bottleneck risk, and that is the point at which a peak premium or member priority tier is genuinely justified rather than merely tempting.
The larger prize sits in the other 60% of weekly demand. Many clubs start near 0–10% off-peak utilisation against a credible medium-term target of 30–50%, and the levers are programmatic rather than tariff-based: leagues, coaching blocks, junior programmes and corporate events that make courts productive beyond the classic weekday evening peak (Padel Business Magazine). Trento's club illustrates it: occupancy improved once lessons began, a targeted university-student campaign kept courts rented in the mornings, and corporate team-building, lessons and club tournaments proved effective at boosting revenue (Padel Business Magazine). That gap — idle daytime and off-peak hours across every court in the building — is capacity that already exists and is currently unsold; closing even part of it moves the needle more than any peak-hour tariff change. Note the structural limit: multi-use and programming buy off-peak utilisation, never additional peak throughput.
5. Risks and weaknesses to manage
Three constraints deserve naming. First, over-indexing on premium hardware: expensive courts are sunk costs that do not necessarily return what players will pay to use them, though high-quality courts remain an indispensable building block — the cheap, high-signal fixes are lighting consistency, ball quality, maintenance, cleanliness and signage (Padel Business Magazine). Second, platform dependency, addressed above. Third, execution capacity: a coaching-led upsell strategy stands or falls on a real coach pipeline — pro-level, federation-certified coaches are what attracts players willing to pay for lessons and drives the retention that follows (Padel Business Magazine); where a club positions coaching as its core stream, it becomes a genuine diversification play rather than an ancillary add-on, and should be resourced and measured accordingly. Ancillary offers such as gym, yoga, physio or beauty should be assessed as separate P&Ls on shared space and shared management — their value is attractiveness and synergy more than direct revenue.
Scheduling discipline underpins all of it: 60–90 minute padel slots, a 7–14 day advance-booking window to prevent hoarding, no-show and late-cancel penalties, and events placed in shoulder or off-peak windows with blocks communicated early, since events during peak reduce member access and drive churn.
6. Verdict
Sequence coaching and membership first, events and sponsorship second, F&B and retail last — and make the premium peak tariff conditional on demonstrated peak utilisation in the 70–85% band. For a membership-led club the highest-return work is tier design, rolling payment cadence and the renewal conversation; for a utilisation-led club it is off-peak programming and yield management, with membership reframed as a pass or pack rather than a club. Under six courts, treat ancillary revenue as retention glue rather than a P&L line; above eight, build the programming stack deliberately, because that is where the margin inflection lives.
Confidence: Medium. The structural logic — scale-dependent revenue mix, journey touchpoints, utilisation gating — is well grounded. Price points, conversion rates and revenue-per-court-hour are highly market-specific and must be validated locally; there are no reliable industry statistics on padel club income mix, though direct fees for court hire, memberships, tournaments and coaching are likely the most important source for most clubs (Padel Business Magazine). The Trento case is seven months old at the time of reporting, and one supporting membership case dates from 2024 with no newer data available. Naming the country and the club's court count would sharpen the pricing and F&B calls materially; this report is directional, not decision-grade on tariffs.
